THURSDAY, MARCH 21, 1946

Displaced Persons From Eastern Front Receive Treatment in U.S. Facilities

German Refugees Among Those Assisted by Medical Aid Program

By ROBERT J. HARRISON
Staff Correspondent

American medical authorities are treating dozens of displaced persons evacuated from the ruins of Eastern Europe, many displaying what doctors describe as "unusual physiological conditions" stemming from their wartime experiences.

Among the most remarkable cases are approximately forty to sixty individuals who survived the 872-day siege of Leningrad, where Soviet citizens endured starvation, bombardment, and temperatures reaching forty degrees below zero. These patients were evacuated through Sweden in late 1945 and early 1946, arriving at medical facilities now under U.S. military administration in occupied Germany.

Dr. Klaus Reinhardt, director of the Frankfurt Klinik für Neuropsychiatrie, confirmed that his facility has received several dozen patients from the Leningrad evacuation. "These individuals have experienced traumas beyond the scope of conventional medical understanding," Dr. Reinhardt stated in an interview. "We are employing both traditional psychiatric methodologies and experimental therapeutic approaches."

The Frankfurt clinic, which came under American oversight following Germany's surrender, has been designated as a specialized treatment center for displaced persons presenting complex psychological and neurological symptoms. Dr. Reinhardt, who served in the Wehrmacht medical corps during the war, was retained by U.S. authorities due to his expertise in treating combat-related psychological trauma.

When pressed for specifics about the "unusual physiological conditions" mentioned in official reports, Dr. Reinhardt declined to provide details, citing patient confidentiality and ongoing medical research. "What I can say is that prolonged exposure to extreme deprivation—both physical and psychological—can manifest in ways that challenge our current medical paradigms," he explained.

The Leningrad survivors represent only a fraction of the estimated eight million displaced persons currently scattered across war-torn Europe. American military authorities, working alongside the newly formed United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, face the enormous challenge of providing care for millions of refugees, concentration camp survivors, and forced laborers freed from Nazi control.

Sources within the military administration indicated that the Frankfurt facility operates under heightened security protocols, though officials emphasized this is standard procedure for installations handling sensitive medical research and vulnerable patient populations.

The clinic is expected to continue operations under American supervision for the foreseeable future, with Dr. Reinhardt remaining as director pending completion of his denazification review. U.S. military spokesman Lt. Col. James Whitmore stated that retaining experienced German medical personnel is necessary "to ensure continuity of care for patients with specialized needs."
